# Industry Angles

Vertical-specific pain points, positioning hooks, and competitive context for proposal customization. When generating a proposal for a specific industry, pull the relevant section and weave its language into the Situation and Approach sections.

If the prospect's industry isn't listed here, extrapolate from the closest match and research their specific market. The pattern is always the same: identify the "1001 competitors" problem, name the specific way they're currently invisible, and show how authority positioning breaks them out of the commodity trap.

## Home services (roofing, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, gutters)

**The core problem:** Every metro area has dozens to hundreds of these businesses. They compete almost exclusively on price and Google reviews. The ones who break out are the ones who become a recognizable name — not just a listing.

**Pain language:**
- "There are [N]+ [roofers/HVAC companies/etc.] in the [City] metro. Right now, a homeowner searching for [service] sees a list of names that all look the same."
- "You're competing on price because you haven't given anyone a reason to choose you for something else."
- "Your best sales tool is word of mouth — but word of mouth doesn't scale."

**Authority positioning angle:** The owner becomes the face. Video content showing real projects, real expertise, real personality. The homeowner hires the person they feel like they already know.

**Content hooks:** Project walkthroughs, "what to look for" educational content, seasonal tips, before/after transformations, "why this costs what it costs" transparency content.

**Ad angle:** Local targeting, direct-response lead gen, "meet your contractor" style video ads that build familiarity before the estimate.

**Proof points to reference:** GE Outdoors (Jason/Tera) — project series concept, $500K revenue target, "one shoot, five channels" leverage model.

## Trades and construction (general contracting, concrete, excavation, framing)

**The core problem:** These businesses are often owner-operated, referral-dependent, and have zero digital presence beyond a basic website or Facebook page. The owner is too busy doing the work to market the work.

**Pain language:**
- "You've built your business on handshakes and referrals. That works — until it doesn't. One slow season and you realize you have no pipeline."
- "Your competitors are winning jobs you should be getting because they show up online and you don't."

**Authority positioning angle:** Document the craftsmanship. Show the scale of projects. Position the owner as someone who takes pride in the work and can explain why quality matters.

**Content hooks:** Time-lapse project videos, "how we built this" breakdowns, equipment and process content, team culture.

## Fitness, coaching, and wellness

**The core problem:** Oversaturated market. Every trainer, coach, and wellness practitioner is posting "motivation Monday" content that looks identical. The ones who win are the ones with a specific methodology and the authority to back it up.

**Pain language:**
- "You're posting content but it's not converting because it looks like everyone else's content."
- "You have expertise that deserves a bigger platform — but right now you're competing with influencers who have better cameras and worse advice."

**Authority positioning angle:** Package the methodology. Name the system. Position the practitioner as the expert who wrote the playbook, not just another trainer with a ring light.

**Content hooks:** Client transformation stories, "why [common advice] is wrong" contrarian takes, methodology breakdowns, behind-the-scenes of actual sessions.

**Proof points to reference:** Dynamic Edge / The Crucible — authority positioning for a sports performance business, splitting athletic training from the business consulting program.

## Woodworking, crafting, and artisan businesses

**The core problem:** Makers who are incredible at their craft but running on razor-thin margins because they've never questioned their supply costs, never positioned themselves above commodity pricing, and never built a brand that justifies premium rates.

**Pain language:**
- "You're clearing [$X] on a good year and calling it a business. It's a really expensive hobby you haven't figured out how to quit yet."
- "You've been loyal to [leading brand] because that's what everybody uses. But what if that loyalty is literally the reason you can't pay yourself what you're worth?"

**Authority positioning angle:** Position the maker as an artist and expert, not a commodity producer. Build content around the craft, the process, the philosophy — which justifies premium pricing.

**Content hooks:** Process videos, material deep-dives, "why this piece costs what it costs" transparency, studio tours, craft philosophy.

**Proof points to reference:** Rustic Lumber Store / Woodaddy — $60K saved for customers, $5 sample offer, call-out style ads targeting small furniture makers.

## Professional services (law, accounting, financial planning, consulting)

**The core problem:** These businesses have high trust barriers. Prospects need to feel confident before they'll even pick up the phone. Most professional service firms have sterile, corporate marketing that says nothing about the actual humans running the practice.

**Pain language:**
- "Your website looks like every other [law firm/accounting firm]. It says 'trusted' and 'experienced' but it doesn't show me why I should trust YOU."
- "People hire people, not firms. But your marketing erases the person and sells the letterhead."

**Authority positioning angle:** Put the principal's face and voice front and center. Thought leadership content that demonstrates expertise on topics prospects are already Googling. Humanize the practice.

**Content hooks:** "What to do if [common situation]" educational series, case study narratives (anonymized where needed), industry commentary, FAQ-style short-form.

## Nonprofits and community organizations

**The core problem:** Lean budgets, skeleton marketing teams (or none), and a story that deserves to be told but isn't being told well. Often rely on grants and donations that are harder to secure without visible community impact.

**Pain language:**
- "You're doing incredible work that nobody outside your immediate circle knows about."
- "Your impact story is your most powerful fundraising tool — but it's trapped in annual reports that nobody reads."

**Authority positioning angle:** Tell the impact stories through video. Show the humans being served. Make donors and community partners feel the work, not just read about it.

**Content hooks:** Client/beneficiary stories (with consent), day-in-the-life of staff, impact by the numbers, community event coverage, volunteer spotlights.

**Proof points to reference:** Midwest Community Services / Midwest Family Services (Amber Crow) — social media management, video production, content strategy for a nonprofit serving individuals with disabilities.

## E-commerce and DTC brands

**The core problem:** Paid acquisition costs are rising. Brands that rely solely on Meta/Google ads without organic authority are trapped in a spend-to-survive cycle. The ones who win build audiences that buy without being targeted.

**Pain language:**
- "Your customer acquisition cost is going up every quarter because you're renting attention instead of owning it."
- "You've got a great product that people love after they buy it — but you're invisible to the 99% who haven't bought yet."

**Authority positioning angle:** Build the brand story. Content that creates affinity before the purchase — behind-the-scenes of production, founder story, user-generated content strategy, community building.

**Content hooks:** Product education, founder story arcs, UGC campaigns, unboxing and review content, "why we made this" origin stories.

**Proof points to reference:** Woodaddy DTC furniture oil campaign — UGC challenge, $5 sample offer, call-out ads targeting specific buyer segments.

## Adapting for unlisted verticals

When the prospect's industry isn't listed above, follow this process:

1. Identify the "1001 competitors" equivalent — how crowded is their market?
2. Name the default way businesses in that industry market themselves (and why it doesn't work)
3. Identify the trust barrier — what does a prospect need to feel before they buy?
4. Determine the authority lever — what kind of content would position the owner as the obvious choice?
5. Find the content hooks — what questions are their prospects already asking?
6. Frame the cost of inaction — what are they losing every month by staying invisible?

The Authority Engine works for any service-based business where the buyer makes a trust-based decision. If the prospect's customer needs to trust someone before spending money, authority positioning is the play.
