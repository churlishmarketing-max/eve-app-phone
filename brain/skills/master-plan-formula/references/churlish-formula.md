# The Churlish Strategic Formula

The methodology behind every Master Plan. This file is the strategist's reference — the *why* and *how* of structuring a business. Read this before writing any plan.

---

## Core thesis

> **Most businesses don't have a lead problem. They have a position problem.**

Lead-gen agencies sell volume. Marketing platforms sell tactics. Operators sell delivery. The Master Plan sells the position from which all of those become inevitable. Get the position right and the leads, tactics, and operations organize themselves around it. Get the position wrong and no amount of ad spend fixes it.

Every Churlish Master Plan opens by naming the position. Every other section is downstream of that.

---

## The 10 sections — what they do strategically

### 01 — The Strategic Bet

**Purpose:** State the business's strategic position in one paragraph.

This is not a mission statement. It's not "what the business does." It's the **strategic bet** — the position the business is taking that competitors aren't, the customer the business is choosing to be wrong for in order to be right for someone else.

Format: 3-5 sentences. Name the market reality, name the prevailing approach, name the contrarian position, name who that position serves.

**Example (Churlish):**
> Service-based businesses don't have a lead problem. They have an authority problem. Lead-gen agencies sell volume. Churlish sells the position from which volume becomes inevitable. We work with founders who have already won — they need a media operation that makes them un-ignorable, not a marketer who'll try to make them likable.

**Example (Roofing company):**
> Most roofing companies compete on price because they have nothing else to compete on. We compete on speed of decision and trust of crew. Our customer doesn't want quotes from four companies — they want one company that can be on the roof by Friday and hand them an answer that doesn't sound like a sales pitch. We charge a premium because that premium pays for the certainty.

### 02 — The Operating Model

**Purpose:** Show the 3-5 pillars (revenue streams or business units) and what each does.

Every business has multiple lines of revenue or activity. Most operators run them all as "the business" without distinguishing roles. The operating model assigns each pillar a **strategic role**:

- **Cash flow pillar** — the predictable monthly revenue that keeps the lights on
- **Anchor pillar** — the premium offer that defines the brand
- **Scale pillar** — the asset that compounds (community, IP, platform)
- **Brand pillar** — the visible work that wins authority
- **Growth pillar** — the new bet not yet proven

Not every business has all five. Most have three. Some have four.

**Example (Churlish):**
- Authority Engine (Anchor) — $3,000–$5,000/mo retainers
- Cutting Room Floor (Cash flow) — $1,250–$3,500/mo subscriptions
- High-Level Pros (Scale) — paid community, Phase 3
- MindCTRL Studios (Brand + Future IP) — original content

**Example (Roofing company):**
- Insurance restoration (Cash flow) — repeatable storm work
- Premium retrofit (Anchor) — high-margin new roofs with extended warranty
- Commercial maintenance (Scale) — recurring contracts with property managers
- "Roof Health" program (Growth) — paid annual inspection subscription

### 03 — Pricing Architecture

**Purpose:** Define 3 tiers per pillar with explicit minimum commitments.

The Churlish default is **on-ramp / core / premium**:

- **On-ramp** — lowest commitment, qualifier offer, 3-month minimum
- **Core** — anchor offer, gets the recommended badge, 6-month minimum
- **Premium** — highest commitment, performance-tied, 6-12 month minimum

Every tier has:
- A name (3-syllable max, memorable)
- A one-line pitch ("what this is for")
- A price (monthly, or project flat)
- A minimum commitment
- 4-6 features (use the `+` plus-sign pattern, not bullets)

**Pricing principles:**
1. Always have a minimum. Discomfort with commitment IS the filter.
2. Never discount month one. Discount month four if needed for retention.
3. Ad spend is separate. Always. $500/mo minimum if relevant to the offer.
4. The middle tier is the anchor. Price it where most should buy. Make it visually dominant.
5. Project work (one-off) is a ladder rung, not a substitute for retainer.

Detailed pricing patterns by business type are in `pricing-architectures.md`.

### 04 — Service Ladder (or Product Ladder)

**Purpose:** Show the customer journey from first dollar to top-tier engagement.

Most businesses have a "main offer" and call it a day. The ladder shows the **path** a customer takes from cold prospect to top-tier client. Three rungs minimum:

**Rung 1: Pre-qualifier** — low-ticket paid offer that filters buyers
- For service: $297–$997 audit, diagnostic, mini-engagement
- For product: $9–$49 entry SKU
- For SaaS: free tier with usage cap
- For creator: $7–$47 lead-magnet upgrade

**Rung 2: Core offer** — the subscription or retainer
- For service: $1,250–$5,000/mo retainer or subscription
- For product: $50–$300 flagship SKU
- For SaaS: $99–$499/mo Pro tier
- For creator: $97–$297/mo membership

**Rung 3: Premium** — the top tier
- For service: $5,000+/mo full engagement or performance partnership
- For product: $500+ premium bundle or annual subscription
- For SaaS: $1,000+/mo Enterprise / custom
- For creator: $2,000+ cohort, mastermind, or 1:1

The ladder is also the **acquisition strategy**. Marketing dollars buy customers onto Rung 1, where they self-select into Rungs 2 and 3 based on fit and capacity to invest.

### 05 — Revenue Model

**Purpose:** Show the math. Don't just state the target.

Format:
```
Target: $X by [date]
Path: [count] × [price] × [duration] = [subtotal]
       [count] × [price] × [duration] = [subtotal]
       [count] × [price]              = [subtotal]
Total: $X
```

If the math doesn't reach the target with realistic assumptions, the **target needs to change** or the **price/offer needs to change** — not the math. Don't fudge. Honesty here is what makes the document useful.

**Example (Churlish 2026):**
- Target: $200K annual revenue
- 10 CRF clients × $1,250 × 9 months = $112,500
- 3 AE retainers × $3,500 × 6 months = $63,000
- Project work blended = $25,000
- Total = $200,500 ✓

Use the `cash-hero` component for the target statement and `metric-table` for the breakdown.

### 06 — KPIs and Tracking

**Purpose:** Define what gets measured. If it's not measured, it doesn't get managed.

Three categories every business tracks:

1. **Revenue KPIs** — MRR, AOV, retention, LTV
2. **Operations KPIs** — delivery time, capacity used, error/rework rate
3. **Marketing KPIs** — CPL, conversion rate, channel mix, ad efficiency

Detailed KPI library by business type in `kpi-library.md`. Pick 6-12 KPIs total for the plan — not more. KPIs that aren't reviewed weekly aren't real.

For each KPI: name, current value (or "TBD" if unknown), target value, review cadence.

### 07 — Gap Analysis

**Purpose:** Name every weak point in the business explicitly.

Most strategy docs hide weakness. The Master Plan exposes it. Three severity levels:

- 🟥 **Red (bleeding)** — actively losing money or customers right now. Highest priority.
- 🟨 **Gold (friction)** — slowing growth or creating frustration. Solve next.
- 🟦 **Teal (noted)** — not urgent but documented for awareness.

Use the `gap-pattern` component with colored swatches.

Common gap categories:
- **Process** — no documented sales process, inconsistent delivery
- **Pricing** — same scope quoted at different prices, no pricing floor
- **Positioning** — unclear ICP, no stated differentiation
- **Capacity** — owner-dependent, no second seat in any function
- **Cash** — no operating reserve, inconsistent collections
- **Pipeline** — referral-only acquisition, no marketing system

Name 5-10 gaps per plan. Don't soft-pedal — clarity here is the value of the document.

### 08 — Roadmap / Phases

**Purpose:** Sequence the work across three time horizons.

Use the **Right Now / Growing / Future** model:

- **Right Now (next 90 days)** — cash flow priorities. What gets done first. Use `rev-now` card with green top border.
- **Growing (90-365 days)** — the anchor offer matures, the ladder is built out, KPIs come online. Use `rev-growing` card with gold top border.
- **Future (year 2-3)** — scale plays, IP, community, new pillars. Use `rev-future` card with red top border.

Each phase gets 3-5 bullet outcomes (not tasks — outcomes). The roadmap is **what will be true**, not what will be done.

### 09 — The Endgame

**Purpose:** State the 3-5 year vision in one declarative sentence.

The endgame is the *why this plan matters*. It's the sentence that ends every internal debate when someone forgets why they're doing the work.

Rules:
- One sentence (long sentence OK, but one).
- Specific (a number, a position, a state — not "be the best")
- Visual (paints a picture, not just states a fact)
- True (the business actually believes this)

Use the `endgame-hero` dark block with the most important word highlighted in gold.

**Example (Churlish):**
> By 2028, Churlish operates a full-service story-performance studio in Omaha with original IP through MindCTRL, a paid community through HLP, and a roster of authority-tier clients across Health, Wealth, and Relationships — the **Hub** of the Midwest's creative economy.

**Example (Roofing company):**
> In three years, the company is the highest-priced and fastest-growing residential roofer in the Tulsa metro, with a 60% commercial maintenance book that holds revenue flat through every weather cycle.

### 10 — Q&A / Internal Notes

**Purpose:** Anticipate the questions the plan will raise and answer them.

Every plan generates objections:
- "Why this pricing?"
- "Why these tiers?"
- "Why not [common alternative]?"
- "What about [competitor]?"
- "How do we afford this?"
- "What if [X] fails?"

Pre-empt 5-8 questions. Use the `qa-block` component. Answers are 2-4 sentences, direct, strategist-voice. Don't soften.

This section is often the most-read part of the doc when the user revisits it in month 3 and forgets *why* they made a decision in month 1.

## Voice and copy notes (Churlish-specific)

Even when writing for a non-Churlish brand, the **document itself** is in the Churlish strategist voice. The Master Plan is a document Brandon (or a Brandon-style strategist) is writing TO the business owner — not what the business says to its customers.

- Direct, conversational, anti-corporate
- Strategist as friend who's seen the inside of 200 businesses
- Names problems before solutions
- Names costs before benefits
- Uses short declarative sentences as section openers
- Uses callouts for emphasis, not bold soup
- No "leverage," "synergy," "move the needle," "best-in-class," "thought leader," "holistic," "ecosystem," "stakeholder alignment"

If writing copy/headlines/offers for inside the plan (e.g., the actual ad scripts the business would run), apply the appropriate brand's voice — not Churlish's. The plan describes what the business should say; it doesn't speak as the business.

## When a section doesn't apply

Some sections might not fit a specific business. Use judgment:

- A solo creator might not have a Gap Analysis with 8 categories — collapse it to 3.
- A pre-revenue startup might not have current KPIs — replace with "KPIs to instrument" with target values only.
- A product business might combine Pricing Architecture and Product Ladder into one section.
- A business with no clear "future" pillar might shrink the Roadmap to two phases.

The 10-section structure is the **maximum**. Real plans collapse to 7-9 sections often. Don't pad.

---

## What this formula is NOT

- It is not a marketing plan. Marketing is downstream of position.
- It is not a financial plan. Financials are downstream of pricing.
- It is not an operations plan. Operations are downstream of offers.
- It is not a list of tactics. Tactics are decisions made *after* the plan exists.

The Master Plan answers: "What kind of business are we?" Tactics, marketing, financials, and operations answer: "How do we do that?" — and those need their own documents (see scope notes in SKILL.md).
