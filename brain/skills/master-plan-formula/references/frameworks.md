# Frameworks

The foundational frameworks embedded in every Churlish Master Plan. These are not the plan itself — they're the underlying mental models that shape pricing, offer construction, positioning, and sales process. Reference these when writing the relevant sections.

---

## Hormozi — Grand Slam Offer

**Source:** Alex Hormozi, $100M Offers

**Core idea:** The offer is the difference between a business that works and one that doesn't. Make the offer so good it would be irrational to refuse.

**The Value Equation:**

```
Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement)
        ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)
```

Increase the numerator, decrease the denominator. Every offer can be reframed by:
- **Increasing dream outcome** — bigger payoff, more specific transformation
- **Increasing perceived likelihood** — guarantees, social proof, demonstrable expertise
- **Decreasing time delay** — faster path to result
- **Decreasing effort/sacrifice** — done-for-you, simpler process

**Where it applies in the Master Plan:**
- Pricing Architecture (Section 03) — each tier's offer should be Grand Slam quality
- Service Ladder (Section 04) — each rung increases perceived value enough to justify the next price jump

**Tactical applications:**
- Stack bonuses that increase perceived value without proportional delivery cost
- Add a guarantee (conditional, results-based, or unconditional)
- Frame outcomes in client's language, not provider's
- Make scarcity real, not fake (limited slots, cohort dates, capacity caps)

## Brunson — Hook, Story, Offer

**Source:** Russell Brunson, Expert Secrets / Dotcom Secrets

**Core idea:** Every piece of marketing follows the same structure: hook the attention, tell a story that builds belief, present the offer.

**Hook:**
- 1-3 seconds to stop the scroll
- Pattern interrupt, curiosity gap, or direct callout
- Must promise resolution by the end

**Story:**
- Bridges the hook to the offer
- Builds belief in the mechanism, the operator, and the result
- Reduces objections through narrative, not argument
- Common structures: hero's journey, before/after, contrarian discovery

**Offer:**
- The Grand Slam Offer (see Hormozi)
- CTA must be specific and low-friction
- Stack value visibly: deliverable × deliverable × bonus = price

**Where it applies in the Master Plan:**
- Service Ladder (Section 04) — each rung is its own hook-story-offer
- Strategic Bet (Section 01) — the document itself follows hook-story-offer (the bet is the hook, the operating model is the story, the pricing is the offer)
- Marketing implications drive what Roadmap (Section 08) and KPIs (Section 06) prioritize

## Voss — Never Split the Difference

**Source:** Chris Voss, FBI hostage negotiator

**Core idea:** Most negotiation advice is wrong. Tactical empathy beats logic. The path to "yes" runs through "no."

**Key tactics:**
- **Mirroring** — repeat the last 1-3 words of what the other person said, with an upward inflection
- **Labeling** — name the other person's emotion ("It sounds like you're worried about commitment")
- **Calibrated questions** — "How am I supposed to do that?" instead of "I can't do that"
- **The accusation audit** — name every negative thing the other person might be thinking before they say it
- **"That's right" vs "You're right"** — the goal is "that's right" (genuine agreement), not "you're right" (placation)
- **Getting to "no"** — let the other person say no early; it makes them feel in control

**Where it applies in the Master Plan:**
- Pricing Architecture (Section 03) — pricing conversations
- Q&A section (Section 10) — the questions section is itself an accusation audit
- Sales process built into the Operating Model

**Tactical applications:**
- Open discovery calls with "It sounds like you've tried [common solution] and it didn't work"
- Use calibrated questions to handle price objections: "How does that compare to what you were budgeting?"
- Get the prospect to articulate the cost of inaction in their own words

## NEPQ — Neuro-Emotional Persuasion Questioning

**Source:** Jeremy Miner, 7th Level

**Core idea:** Modern buyers are immune to traditional sales tactics. Lead with questions that surface and amplify the prospect's existing pain. Position the solution only after the prospect has articulated the gap themselves.

**The NEPQ flow:**

1. **Connecting questions** — establish rapport without small talk
2. **Situation questions** — understand current state
3. **Problem awareness questions** — surface the gaps the prospect already feels
4. **Solution awareness questions** — explore what they've tried
5. **Consequence questions** — quantify the cost of inaction
6. **Qualifying questions** — establish ability and authority to act
7. **Transition** — frame the offer as the natural conclusion of what they've described

**Key principle:** "Tell don't sell." The seller asks; the buyer convinces themselves.

**Where it applies in the Master Plan:**
- Discovery call structure built into Marketing & Acquisition KPIs (Section 06)
- Gap Analysis (Section 07) is essentially the consequence question applied to the business itself
- Service Ladder rung 1 (pre-qualifier audit) functions as a NEPQ-style filter

**Tactical applications:**
- Replace "Tell me about your business" with "What's the most frustrating part of how you're currently doing this?"
- Replace pitch with "If we could solve X, what would that be worth to you?"
- Use consequence questions to anchor pricing: "If you stay where you are for another year, what does that cost?"

## How these frameworks combine in the Master Plan

The four frameworks operate at different layers:

| Framework | Layer | Applies to |
|-----------|-------|-----------|
| Hormozi | Offer construction | Pricing Architecture, Service Ladder |
| Brunson | Narrative structure | Marketing messaging, Strategic Bet, Endgame |
| Voss | Sales conversation | Discovery calls, pricing negotiations, retention conversations |
| NEPQ | Sales process | Discovery scripts, qualification, consequence-anchoring |

A Master Plan that doesn't apply at least 2 of these has a gap. A Master Plan that names all 4 explicitly is stronger.

## Other frameworks worth knowing

These aren't required reading but show up in adjacent contexts:

- **Jobs-to-be-Done (Christensen)** — customers hire products to do specific jobs. Useful for ICP definition and offer positioning.
- **StoryBrand (Donald Miller)** — variation of hook-story-offer with the customer as hero, brand as guide. Useful for messaging.
- **Blue Ocean Strategy (Kim & Mauborgne)** — competing on different attributes rather than the same ones, harder. Useful for Strategic Bet.
- **The E-Myth (Michael Gerber)** — working ON the business, not IN it. Useful for Gap Analysis around owner-dependency.
- **Profit First (Mike Michalowicz)** — operating cash flow rules. Useful for financial gap analysis.
- **Lean Startup (Eric Ries)** — minimum viable testing, build-measure-learn. Useful for Right Now phase of pre-revenue businesses.

If the brain-dump reveals a specific pattern (e.g., owner working IN the business too much), nodding to the relevant framework in the Q&A section adds authority without preaching.

## When NOT to cite frameworks

The Master Plan is a strategist's document, not a textbook. Frameworks inform the *thinking* — they don't need to be named explicitly unless naming them helps the reader.

Cite a framework when:
- The reader will immediately recognize it (Hormozi, Voss are widely known)
- The framework name shorthand-communicates a complex idea
- The Q&A section is addressing a question that the framework answers

Don't cite when:
- The framework is more obscure and citation requires explanation
- The point can be made cleaner without the citation
- The reader is unlikely to have read the source

Default: apply the frameworks, don't name them. Let the structure speak.
