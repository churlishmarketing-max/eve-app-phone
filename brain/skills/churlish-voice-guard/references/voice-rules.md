# Churlish Media Voice Rules

## The voice in one sentence

Churlish Media sounds like a sharp, confident friend who happens to know more about marketing than anyone you've met — and isn't afraid to tell you what's actually wrong before selling you the fix.

## Core principles

### 1. Direct over diplomatic

Say the uncomfortable thing first. Churlish copy opens with the problem, not the pleasantry. The reader should feel seen — and slightly called out — within the first two sentences.

**Yes:**
"You're running a $25,000 furniture business and pretending you can't afford to know what your finish is actually costing you."

**No:**
"Many small business owners struggle with understanding their true costs, which can impact their bottom line over time."

### 2. Conversational authority

Write like you're talking to someone across a table, not presenting to a boardroom. Use contractions. Use "you" and "your." Ask rhetorical questions. But never sacrifice substance for casualness — the authority comes from knowing the numbers, not from slang.

**Yes:**
"Here's why. The average small furniture maker doing $20K–$30K a year is operating on margins between 20 and 35%. That means after wood, tools, shop costs, and finish — you're clearing somewhere between $4,000 and $10,500 on a good year. That's not a business. That's a really expensive hobby you haven't figured out how to quit yet."

**No:**
"Industry data suggests that profit margins for small-scale artisan businesses typically range from 20-35%, which can significantly impact annual take-home revenue."

### 3. Proof before promise

Never claim a result without evidence. Use specific numbers, specific client names (when permitted), and specific timelines. "We get results" is meaningless. "In 2025 we saved our customers over $60K by switching from the leading brand" is a weapon.

**Yes:**
"47 leads in 21 days."
"La Keshia went from invisible to industry authority in 90 days."
"Jason's $21K story."

**No:**
"We've helped numerous clients achieve significant growth."
"Our proven track record speaks for itself."

### 4. Tension before resolution

The best Churlish copy creates a gap between where the reader is and where they want to be — then holds it open long enough for them to feel it before offering the bridge. Don't rush to the solution. Let the problem breathe.

Structure: Problem (make them feel it) → Pivot (reframe why it exists) → Proof (show it's solvable) → Offer (hand them the key)

### 5. Anti-corporate, pro-substance

Churlish Media exists in opposition to the bloated agency model. The copy should reflect that. No jargon without justification. No buzzwords as substitutes for ideas. If a sentence could appear in any agency's deck unchanged, it's not Churlish.

**Banned phrases (never use these):**
- "Leverage" (as a verb)
- "Synergy" / "synergistic"
- "Move the needle"
- "Touch base"
- "Circle back"
- "Best-in-class"
- "Cutting-edge"
- "End-to-end solution"
- "Holistic approach"
- "Thought leader" (use "authority" — it's the whole brand)
- "Disrupt" / "disruptive"
- "Empower" (unless used with teeth — i.e., followed by a specific outcome)
- "At the end of the day"
- "It goes without saying"
- "In today's competitive landscape"
- "Take your business to the next level"

**Churlish replacements:**
- Instead of "leverage" → "use" or "put to work"
- Instead of "optimize" → "fix" or "tighten up"
- Instead of "ROI" → the actual dollar amount or percentage
- Instead of "engagement" → "people actually watching / clicking / buying"
- Instead of "deliverables" (in client copy) → name the specific things
- Instead of "scalable" → describe what actually happens when they grow

## The call-out style

This is the signature Churlish format for direct-response ads and social content. Structure:

1. **Hook / pattern interrupt** — Bold, provocative opening that stops the scroll. Usually a direct challenge or uncomfortable truth. Often formatted as a standalone bold line.

2. **The call-out** — Extended pain identification. Use specific numbers, real industry data, and language that mirrors how the prospect actually talks about their problem. This section should feel like reading someone's private thoughts out loud.

3. **The pivot** — Reframe why the problem exists. Not "you're dumb" but "you've been told the wrong thing" or "nobody showed you a better way." This shifts blame from the reader to the status quo.

4. **The proof** — Specific results, specific names, specific timelines. One strong case study is worth more than ten vague claims.

5. **The offer** — Clear, tangible, low-friction. State exactly what they get and what it costs. No hidden steps, no "schedule a call to find out pricing."

6. **CTA** — One action. Not three. One. Make it impossible to misunderstand what to do next.

## Tone calibration by deliverable type

### Proposals
Confident but not cocky. You're diagnosing their situation, not pitching them. The proposal should read like a doctor explaining what's wrong and what the treatment plan looks like — authoritative, specific, and reassuring without being soft. Use section headers that name the outcome, not the activity ("Your authority positioning" not "Our content strategy").

### Ad scripts
Maximum call-out energy. Pattern interrupt hard, hold the tension, resolve with proof and an irresistible offer. The first 3 seconds / first line must earn the next 10.

### Email sequences
Warm but purposeful. Every email has one job and one CTA. Use the Chris Voss mirroring and labeling technique — acknowledge where they might be mentally before asking them to do something. Emails should feel like they're from a person, not a funnel.

### Strategy documents
Clear and comprehensive. These can be longer and more structured. Use tables, frameworks, and specific numbers. The tone is "here's exactly what we're going to do and why" — strategic confidence without the fluff.

### Social captions
Short, punchy, scroll-stopping. Open with a hook. No hashtag spam. CTAs should feel natural, not forced. Captions should feel like overhearing something interesting, not reading an ad.

### Video scripts
Write for the ear, not the page. Short sentences. Pauses built in. The script should feel like Brandon talking, not Brandon reading.

## Frameworks referenced in Churlish copy

These influence how Churlish structures arguments, handles objections, and closes:

- **Alex Hormozi (Grand Slam Offer):** The offer should be so good that people feel stupid saying no. Stack value, reduce risk, name the specific outcomes.
- **Russell Brunson (Expert Secrets / DotCom Secrets):** Story-driven selling. Every piece of content follows a hook → story → offer arc. The Attractive Character framework shapes how Brandon shows up.
- **Chris Voss (Never Split the Difference):** Tactical empathy in follow-ups and objection handling. Mirror their words. Label their feelings. Use calibrated questions ("How am I supposed to do that?"). The "that's right" moment is the goal of every objection response.
- **NEPQ (New Economy Professional Questioning):** The discovery call framework. Questions that lead the prospect to their own conclusions rather than being told what to think.

## The "Would Brandon say this?" test

Before finalizing any copy, read it out loud. If it sounds like:
- A LinkedIn post from a marketing agency → Rewrite
- A corporate pitch deck → Rewrite
- A BuzzFeed listicle → Rewrite
- Something Brandon would say on a podcast while leaning forward → Ship it
