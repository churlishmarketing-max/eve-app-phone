# Hook Library

Hook formulas and pattern interrupt techniques organized by psychological trigger. Each formula includes the structure, why it works, and examples from Churlish campaigns.

The hook is the single most important element of any ad. If the hook fails, nothing else matters. Every hook in this library has been designed to pass the "thumb test" — would someone scrolling at full speed on their phone stop for this?

## Hook formula categories

### 1. The direct call-out

**Formula:** "You're [doing/being something specific] and [uncomfortable consequence]."

**Why it works:** Calls the viewer out by name (or by behavior). Creates the feeling of being seen. The specificity is what makes it stop — generic call-outs ("Attention business owners!") get ignored because they could be for anyone.

**Examples:**
- "You're running a $25,000 furniture business and pretending you can't afford to know what your finish is actually costing you."
- "You're posting content three times a week and getting fewer leads than the guy who posted once last month."
- "You've got 47 five-star reviews and you're still competing on price. That's not a marketing problem — that's a positioning problem."

**When to use:** TOF ads where you need to establish "I'm talking to YOU specifically." Works best when you can include a specific number or detail that proves you know their world.

### 2. The uncomfortable truth

**Formula:** "[Thing everyone believes] is [actually wrong/costing them]."

**Why it works:** Challenges a belief the viewer holds, which creates cognitive dissonance — they have to resolve it, which means they keep watching/reading.

**Examples:**
- "Your marketing agency is the most expensive employee you'll never fire."
- "The reason your ads aren't working has nothing to do with your ads."
- "Consistency isn't the problem. You're consistently doing the wrong thing."

**When to use:** TOF and MOF. Strong for contrarian positioning. Sets up the pivot where you explain the real cause of their problem.

### 3. The specific result

**Formula:** "[Specific number or outcome]. [Short context]."

**Why it works:** Numbers stop the scroll. A specific result is inherently more believable than a vague claim. The viewer wants to know how.

**Examples:**
- "47 leads in 21 days. Here's exactly what we did."
- "In 2025, we saved our customers over $60,000 by switching from the leading brand."
- "$21,000 in new revenue from one video series. No paid ads."

**When to use:** MOF and BOF. Proof-driven hooks for audiences who already know the category. Pairs well with case study or testimonial content behind it.

### 4. The provocative question

**Formula:** "[Question that the viewer can't scroll past without answering in their head]?"

**Why it works:** Questions trigger automatic mental processing — the brain starts answering before the viewer decides whether to keep scrolling. The question must have an obvious (wrong) answer that you'll challenge, or it must touch a raw nerve.

**Examples:**
- "How many customers did you lose this month because they couldn't find you online?"
- "What's your finish actually costing you per piece?"
- "When was the last time someone hired you because of your Instagram?"

**When to use:** TOF. Opens a loop that the rest of the ad closes. Avoid yes/no questions — they let the viewer answer and leave. Use questions that require thought.

### 5. The "stop doing this" command

**Formula:** "Stop [thing they're doing that's hurting them]."

**Why it works:** Direct, commanding, and slightly confrontational. The viewer wants to know what they're doing wrong — and whether it applies to them.

**Examples:**
- "Stop letting your finish eat your profit."
- "Stop posting content that nobody asked for."
- "Stop competing on price. You're better than that."

**When to use:** TOF and BOF. Strong for retargeting (they've already seen your content, now you're pushing harder). Works well as text overlay on video.

### 6. The "if/then" conditional

**Formula:** "If you're [specific situation], [this is what's actually happening]."

**Why it works:** Self-selection. The viewer decides "yes, that's me" and leans in because the ad has identified their exact situation.

**Examples:**
- "If you're a roofer in Omaha doing under $500K, you're invisible to 90% of your potential customers."
- "If you've been posting for 6 months and still aren't getting leads, it's not a content problem — it's a strategy problem."
- "If your best marketing channel is word of mouth, you don't have marketing. You have luck."

**When to use:** TOF. Powerful for niche targeting because the conditional acts as a qualifier — irrelevant viewers self-select out, relevant viewers feel personally addressed.

### 7. The pattern interrupt (visual/structural)

**Formula:** Open with something unexpected — a visual, a statement, or a format break that doesn't match what the viewer expects to see in their feed.

**Why it works:** The brain is wired to notice anomalies. When everything in a social feed follows the same pattern (talking head, lifestyle shot, product image), something structurally different — a bold text card, a zoom into an unusual detail, a silent pause before speaking — creates a pattern interrupt.

**Examples:**
- Opening on an extreme close-up of hands working wood, with no text and no voiceover for 2 seconds, then pulling back to reveal the full workshop with the hook delivered
- A static ad that's just white text on black: "Your agency won't tell you this."
- Starting a video mid-sentence as if the camera just caught something: "—and that's the part nobody talks about."

**When to use:** Anywhere, but especially TOF where competition for attention is highest. Combine with another hook formula — the pattern interrupt gets attention, the formula keeps it.

### 8. The social proof stack

**Formula:** "[Name] did [specific result]. [Name] did [specific result]. Here's what they all have in common."

**Why it works:** Multiple proof points create the impression of a pattern, not a fluke. The "what they have in common" creates curiosity.

**Examples:**
- "La Keshia went from invisible to industry authority in 90 days. Jason added $21K in new revenue from one video series. Zach saved his customers $60K in one year. Here's what they all have in common."

**When to use:** MOF. Best for audiences who are aware of you but haven't committed. The stack overcomes the "that was a one-time thing" objection.

## Hook variations for A/B testing

When the user asks for "hook variations" or "A/B test options," produce 3–5 hooks using different formulas from this library for the same product/avatar. Label each with the formula used so the user can track which psychological trigger performs best.

**Example set for a roofing company:**

| Variation | Formula | Hook |
|-----------|---------|------|
| A | Direct call-out | "There are 147 roofers in the Omaha metro. Your customers can't tell you apart from 146 of them." |
| B | Uncomfortable truth | "Your five-star Google reviews aren't bringing in new customers. Here's why." |
| C | Specific result | "47 new leads in 21 days — for a roofer who was getting 3 a month." |
| D | Provocative question | "When a homeowner needs a roof, why would they call you instead of the guy they've been watching on YouTube for six months?" |
| E | If/then conditional | "If you're a roofer spending money on ads but not on content, you're paying for attention you can't keep." |

## Hook writing rules

1. **Specificity beats cleverness.** A specific number, name, or detail is always more powerful than a clever turn of phrase. "$60,000 saved" beats "massive savings."

2. **The hook is not the ad.** The hook's only job is to earn the next 5 seconds. Don't try to sell in the hook — sell in the body.

3. **Write 10, keep 3.** When generating hooks, write at least twice as many as you'll use. The best hooks are never the first ones you write.

4. **Read it out loud.** If you stumble while speaking it, the viewer will stumble while reading it. Hooks should be one breath, one thought.

5. **Test the "so what?" filter.** After writing a hook, ask "so what?" as a skeptical stranger. If the answer is "nothing, I'd keep scrolling," rewrite it.

6. **Never start with the brand name.** Nobody stops scrolling for "Churlish Media presents..." The brand earns attention through the hook, not the other way around.

7. **Avoid question hooks that can be answered "no."** "Want more leads?" — no, scroll. "Are you tired of..." — no, scroll. The question must be one they can't dismiss.
